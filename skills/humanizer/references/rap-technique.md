# Rap technique engine

Use this reference for rap, melodic rap, drill, trap, boom-bap, battle rap, freestyle-style writing, or any lyric task where the user cares about bars, cadence, rhyme density, pocket, multis, or flow.

The goal is not maximum technicality. Technical devices must serve the line, the voice, and the beat. A verse with fewer rhymes but better pocket and meaning is stronger than a dense verse that sounds written from a rhyme dictionary.

## 1. Stress map before syllable count

Rap cadence is organized by stressed syllables more than raw syllable totals. Before changing a line:

1. Read it as natural speech.
2. Mark the content words that carry stress.
3. Identify which stresses should land on strong beats or subdivisions.
4. Move filler words before changing the content words.
5. Preserve conversational pronunciation, contractions, dropped syllables, and slang when they fit the voice.

Do not equalize every bar to the same syllable count. Two bars can sit in the same pocket with different syllable totals if their stressed syllables land similarly.

## 2. Pocket

**Pocket** is the recurring placement of stressed syllables and phrase boundaries against the beat. Treat it as a rhythmic shape, not a syllable quota.

When revising for pocket:

- keep a stable anchor pattern for a few bars before changing it;
- allow pickup syllables before beat 1;
- use rests and held vowels as deliberate space;
- vary subdivisions without losing the listener's sense of where the main stresses land;
- avoid cramming unstressed connector words just to preserve a written rhyme;
- let a punchline or emotional detail break the established pocket when the break adds emphasis.

If no beat/BPM is supplied, optimize for spoken rhythmic plausibility rather than pretending to know exact timing.

## 3. Cadence

Cadence is the audible pattern of stress, pause, acceleration, vowel length, and line ending across multiple bars.

Check:

- where the voice naturally speeds up or slows down;
- whether every bar ends with the same downward cadence;
- whether sentence boundaries always coincide with bar boundaries;
- whether enjambment creates forward motion;
- whether pauses occur where a performer could actually breathe;
- whether a repeated cadence is intentional enough to feel like a motif instead of a template.

A useful verse often establishes a cadence, repeats it enough to make it legible, then varies one element at a time.

## 4. Internal multisyllabic rhyme

A multisyllabic rhyme matches a sequence of two or more stressed/unstressed sound units, not necessarily identical spelling.

Prefer sound families over exact words. Compare pronunciation, especially stressed vowels and nearby consonants.

Example technique, not a phrase to reuse:
- anchor sound: `BACK in the DAY`
- family: `PACKED it aWAY`, `CRACK in the PAINT`, `MAP of the PLACE`

The match can be loose if the cadence and vowel contour connect the phrases.

When building internal multis:

1. Choose an anchor phrase that already belongs in the sentence.
2. Find sound-compatible phrases that also advance meaning.
3. Place rhymes at more than one position in the bar when natural: early internal, mid-bar, and/or end rhyme.
4. Let the chain evolve by one sound at a time instead of forcing the exact same vowel/consonant pattern indefinitely.
5. Break the chain when semantic quality starts dropping.

Never replace a concrete fact with a weaker abstraction just to keep the multisyllabic chain alive.

## 5. Slant rhyme and phonetic families

Treat rhyme as a spectrum:

- exact rhyme: same stressed vowel and close ending consonants;
- vowel rhyme/assonance: stressed vowels match while consonants differ;
- consonance: consonant skeleton echoes while vowels shift;
- near/slant rhyme: enough phonetic overlap to feel related in performance;
- mosaic rhyme: several short words rhyme with one longer phrase.

Use slant rhyme to preserve conversational diction. Exact rhyme is valuable when it lands naturally, not as the default target for every bar.

## 6. Rhyme density

Rhyme density measures how much phonetic recurrence appears relative to the amount of language. Do not maximize it blindly.

Estimate three layers:

- **end-rhyme density:** how often line endings rhyme;
- **internal-rhyme density:** recurring sounds inside lines;
- **chain length:** how many bars sustain related phonetic material.

A technically dense verse can still feel human when density rises and falls. Use lower density for setup, narrative, or vulnerable detail; increase it for acceleration, aggression, flexing, punchlines, or a deliberate technical passage.

Flag `RHYME_OVER_CONTENT` when additional rhymes stop adding meaning or force unnatural syntax.

## 7. Rhyme placement

Avoid a verse where every important rhyme appears at the final word. Mix:

- early-bar internal rhyme;
- mid-bar rhyme;
- phrase-end rhyme before the actual bar end;
- end rhyme;
- cross-bar rhyme, where the sound resolves in the next bar;
- repeated vowel families without a full rhyme.

Moving rhyme away from the bar ending often reduces the nursery-rhyme/AABB feel while keeping technical density.

## 8. Semantic progression

Every bar should do at least one useful job:

- add a new event, object, action, consequence, or observation;
- sharpen the speaker's stance;
- change the listener's understanding of an earlier detail;
- set up or land a punchline;
- create musical tension/release;
- advance a rhyme chain without sacrificing meaning.

If two consecutive bars say the same thing in different words, the second is probably an explanation bar or filler.

## 9. Setup and payoff

A punchline does not need a joke. It can be a reversal, concrete reveal, threat, confession, comparison, or unexpected consequence.

Strong setup/payoff usually works because the setup has independent meaning. Avoid obvious placeholder setups whose only purpose is delivering the rhyme word in the next line.

For serious personal writing, do not turn every 2 or 4 bars into a punchline. Let some details land without a clever ending.

## 10. Conversational syntax

A technically dense rap should still sound speakable.

Prefer:
- contractions;
- natural word order;
- sentence fragments that a person would actually say;
- interruptions and parentheticals when the voice uses them;
- rhyme inside normal syntax.

Flag inversions such as unusual adjective/noun order, archaic wording, or unnecessary prepositional phrases when they exist mainly to force rhyme.

## 11. Flow variation

Use variation intentionally across a section:

- change subdivision density;
- move the first stressed syllable earlier or later;
- shorten one bar after several dense bars;
- carry a sentence across the bar line;
- insert a rest before a key phrase;
- repeat one cadence as a motif and then break it.

Do not change all dimensions at once. A listener needs enough repetition to recognize the flow before the variation feels meaningful.

## 12. Hook technique

A hook usually needs lower lexical density than a verse. Prioritize:

- a memorable stress pattern;
- vowel shapes that can be repeated comfortably;
- one central idea rather than a compressed summary of the whole song;
- repetition with a musical reason;
- wording the speaker would plausibly repeat out loud.

Do not make the hook generic just because it is simpler. Simplicity and specificity can coexist.

## 13. Melodic rap

For melodic rap, check the mouth-feel of the vowels on sustained notes. Open vowels tend to sustain more comfortably than dense consonant clusters. Preserve pronunciation and meaning first, then adjust wording to make held vowels singable.

Do not overpack internal rhyme into a melody that needs space.

## 14. Genre calibration

Use the user's sample and brief rather than stereotypes.

- Drill may favor clipped phrasing, repeated pockets, menace, local diction, or sparse punchlines, but none is mandatory.
- Boom-bap may tolerate denser multis and longer syntax, but do not make it encyclopedic by default.
- Trap may use repetition and pocket as primary devices, but repeated phrasing still needs musical purpose.
- Freestyle-style writing may favor associative movement and chained sounds, but should not become random-object filler unless requested.

Never fabricate regional slang, gang affiliation, crimes, drug use, brands, locations, or personal history to make a verse feel authentic.

## Rap analysis output

When the user asks for technical analysis, report only useful metrics and observations. A compact format is:

- **Pocket:** where the main stresses land and where the flow changes.
- **Cadence:** repeated rhythmic phrase shapes and variations.
- **End rhyme:** scheme or loose pattern.
- **Internal rhyme:** notable phonetic chains and positions.
- **Multis:** approximate multi-syllable sound families.
- **Density:** low / medium / high by section, with explanation.
- **Semantic movement:** whether each bar adds information.
- **Weak spots:** forced rhyme, prose cadence, filler, or repeated syntax.

Do not claim exact BPM alignment, bar timing, or stressed-beat placement unless audio, a beat grid, or enough timing information is actually available.

## Final rap rewrite check

Before returning the verse:

- Read every bar aloud as speech.
- Check that rhyme follows meaning rather than replacing it.
- Check that at least some rhymes occur internally instead of only at line ends.
- Verify multis by sound, not spelling.
- Remove words added only to fill meter.
- Keep density uneven enough to breathe.
- Preserve the writer's natural slang, profanity, and roughness.
- Remove any invented personal detail.
- Make sure the verse has semantic movement, not four versions of the same claim.
